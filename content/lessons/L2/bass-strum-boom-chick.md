Instead of strumming the whole chord, pick a single bass note on beats 1 and 3 ("boom") and strum the thinner strings on 2 and 4 ("chick"). It's the sound of country and folk accompaniment, and the bridge from strumming to fingerstyle.

## How to practise this

- 2 minutes: G only, slowly, saying "boom-chick" aloud.
- 4 minutes: the exercise, from 52 BPM up to 80.
- Review: 2 minutes of D – DU – UDU on G, C and D.

## The bass notes

The first boom is the chord's **root**, its home note. The second jumps to another bass string of the same chord, which makes the bass line move:

- **G:** string 6 (fret 3), then string 4 (open).
- **C:** string 5 (fret 3), then string 4 (fret 2).
- **D:** string 4 (open), then string 5 (open).
- **Em:** string 6 (open), then string 5 (fret 2).

::chords{ids="G,C,D,Em"}

## The chick

On beats 2 and 4, a short, light down-strum on the three thinnest strings. Keep it crisp: don't dig into the bass strings.

```atex
\tempo 70
:4 3.6{ch "G"} (0.3 0.2 3.1) 0.4 (0.3 0.2 3.1)
```

::exercise{id="boom-chick-g-c-d-em"}

:::tip
Later, in Level 3, the thumb takes over the booms and the fingers the chicks. That's the alternating bass.
:::

:::warning
Common mistakes:

- Missing the bass string. Look once, then feel the distance between strings.
- A loud chick that swallows the bass. The boom is the star.
- Lifting the chord between booms. Hold the full shape so everything rings.

:::

**Mastery check:** a clean pass of the exercise at 80 BPM.
