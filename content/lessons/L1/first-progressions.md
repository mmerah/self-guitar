A progression is a short series of chords that repeats, and most songs are built from one or two of them. Now you'll chain four chords and keep the loop going.

## How to practise this

- 1 minute of C–D changes, the hardest pair in the loop.
- 4 minutes of the G–Em–C–D exercise, from 46 BPM up to 70.
- Five minutes of one loop beats one minute each of five loops.
- Review: 2 minutes of the Drunken Sailor exercise.

## G – Em – C – D

One of the most common loops in pop and folk.

::chords{ids="G,Em,C,D"}

::exercise{id="progression-g-em-c-d"}

**G to Em, with an anchor:** for this change, finger Em with the **index** on fret 2 of the A string and the **middle** on fret 2 of the D string (the diagram shows middle and ring; both work). In G, the index is already on fret 2 of the A string, so it stays put: lift the other fingers and place the middle on the D string.

**C to D** has no shared finger, so practise it on its own.

::tool[C to D changes]{name="changes" a="C" b="D"}

## More loops to try

All of these use only Level 1 chords:

- **A – D – E – A**: three chords, key of A.
- **G – C – D – G**: the same idea in G.
- **Am – C – D – Am**: a minor-key loop.
- **C – Am – G – G**: uses the C–Am shortcut.

::tool{name="metronome" bpm=60}

When a loop feels easy, stop looking at your fretting hand. Glance at the next chord name instead.

:::warning
Common mistakes:

- Stopping at the end of the loop. Go straight from D back to G, with no gap.
- Strumming all six strings on C and D. Aim the first strum at the right bass string.
- Practising only the easy changes. Isolate the slowest pair with the changes timer.

:::

**Mastery check:** a clean pass of the G–Em–C–D exercise at 70 BPM.
