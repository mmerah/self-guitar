Even strums keep time; uneven strums make a groove. Playing some strums louder than others (accents) and changing the overall volume (dynamics) is what makes a strummed guitar sound alive.

## How to practise this

- 2 minutes: quarter strums on G with the backbeat accent, at 70.
- 2 minutes: D – DU – UDU with accents.
- 1 minute: soft-loud-soft on the same loop. Watch for speeding up when it gets loud.
- Review: 1 minute of Am–E or C–G changes.

## The backbeat

In most pop, folk and rock, beats **2 and 4** are stressed: that's where a drummer hits the snare. Strum those a little harder.

```atex
\tempo 70
:4 (3.6 2.5 0.4 0.3 0.2 3.1){bd sd ch "G"} (3.6{ac} 2.5{ac} 0.4{ac} 0.3{ac} 0.2{ac} 3.1{ac}){bd sd} (3.6 2.5 0.4 0.3 0.2 3.1){bd sd} (3.6{ac} 2.5{ac} 0.4{ac} 0.3{ac} 0.2{ac} 3.1{ac}){bd sd}
```

The ">" marks show the accented strums. Count "1 **2** 3 **4**" and lean on the bold numbers.

## Accents in D – DU – UDU

In the pattern, the natural accents fall on **beat 2** and on the **up-strum after the missed beat 3**. That second accent, on an up-strum, gives the pattern its lift.

::strum{pattern="D - D U - U D U" bpm=70 chord="C"}

## Dynamics

Dynamics is the volume of the whole passage. Songs often have a quiet verse and a louder chorus. Practise a loop four times softly (strum near the neck, light touch), then four times loudly (near the bridge, more arm), without changing the tempo.

::tool{name="metronome" bpm=70 sub=2}

:::warning
Common mistakes:

- Accenting by squeezing the pick. Accent with a slightly bigger, faster arm motion instead.
- Rushing the accented strum. It's louder, not earlier.
- Making the quiet strums so soft they disappear. The difference should be clear but gentle.

:::
