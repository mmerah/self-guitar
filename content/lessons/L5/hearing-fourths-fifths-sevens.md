The 4th, the 5th and the octave are the strong notes of every blues: hear them and you can find the root of a phrase. Goal: name P4, P5 and P8, rising, falling or together, 8 times out of 10.

## How to practise this

- 2 minutes: play each interval below from A, and sing it.
- 4 minutes: the interval quiz until 20 answers are 80% right.
- 2 minutes: the 7-chord quiz.
- Review: one pass of box 1 at 80.

## Three strong intervals

All three are in box 1, from the root A on string 6:

- **4th (P4):** A to D, string 5 fret 5. "Here comes the bride".
- **5th (P5):** A to E, string 5 fret 7. Open and hollow, like a power chord.
- **Octave (P8):** A to A, string 4 fret 7. The same note, higher.

```atex
\tempo 70
:4 5.6 5.5 :2 (5.6 5.5) | :4 5.6 7.5 :2 (5.6 7.5) | :4 5.6 7.4 :2 (5.6 7.4)
```

## The 7 chord

The dominant 7 is a major chord plus the ♭7, the note a whole step below the octave. It sounds bluesy and restless, and it's every chord in a 12-bar blues. Compare A and A7:

::tip[In a 4th the top note wants to fall back; in a 5th both notes sound stable. That's often the quickest way to tell them apart.]

:::warning
Common mistakes:

- Confusing the 4th and the 5th. Sing the scale up from the low note: 1-2-3-4 or 1-2-3-4-5.
- Only practising rising intervals. The quiz mixes directions.
- Calling a 7 chord minor because it sounds dark. Listen for the bright major 3rd underneath.

:::
