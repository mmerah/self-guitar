Numbers make keys easy to move: if a song is I–IV–V in G, it's I–IV–V in any key. Goal: transpose a chord chart, and use a capo to play it in any key with shapes you like.

## How to practise this

- 3 minutes: transpose the three charts below on paper, then play them.
- 3 minutes: one song from the library with the capo on frets 2, 3 and 5; name the sounding key each time.
- Review: 1 minute of I–V–vi–IV in A and E.

## Transposing by numbers

1. Find the key (usually the first and last chord).
2. Write each chord as a number: in G, C is IV and Em is vi.
3. Read the numbers in the new key. IV in D is G; vi in D is Bm.

Try it: G – Em – C – D in G is I – vi – IV – V. In D it's D – Bm – G – A; in A it's A – F♯m – D – E.

## Transposing by counting

Or count half steps along the musical alphabet: A, B♭, B, C, C♯, D, E♭, E, F, F♯, G, A♭. To move up two, every chord moves two places: G → A, C → D, Em → F♯m. The chord quality (m, 7, sus) never changes.

## The capo for any key

A capo moves the open shapes up without changing your fingers. To play in a key you can't reach with open chords:

1. Choose shapes you like (G shapes are easy to sing over).
2. Count half steps from the shape's key up to the key you need: G to B♭ is 3.
3. Capo on that fret: capo 3, G shapes, sounding in B♭.

Silent Night in the library is written with G shapes and capo 1: it sounds in A♭.

::song[Silent Night, capo 1]{id="silent-night" tempo=90}

::chords{ids="G,C,D,Em"}

::tip[Singers: if a song is too low, capo up; if it's too high, try the shapes of a lower key (D instead of G) with the capo.]

:::warning
Common mistakes:

- Changing the chord quality when transposing. Em in G becomes F♯m in A, never F♯.
- Forgetting that B–C and E–F are only one half step apart.
- Reading a capo chart's chord names as the sounding chords. "Capo 3: G" means a G shape sounding as B♭.

:::

**Mastery check:** tick the three statements when they're true for you.
