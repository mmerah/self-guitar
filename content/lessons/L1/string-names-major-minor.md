Two small pieces of knowledge make every later lesson easier: the names of the open strings, and the sound of major versus minor.

## How to practise this

- Each day, say the string names aloud as you tune: "E, A, D, G, B, E".
- 2 minutes: play A or Am (or E or Em) at random with your eyes shut, ten times, and guess.
- 3 minutes: the minor-key exercise at the bottom, listening to each chord's colour.
- Review: 1 minute of Em–Am changes.

## The open strings

From the thickest (string 6) to the thinnest (string 1): **E A D G B E**.

- String 6: low **E**
- String 5: **A**
- String 4: **D**
- String 3: **G**
- String 2: **B**
- String 1: high **e** (written lowercase so you can tell them apart)

```atex
\tempo 70
:4 0.6 0.5 0.4 0.3 | 0.2 0.1 :2 r
```

The two E strings are two **octaves** apart. An octave is the distance from one note to the next note with the same name: it sounds like the same note, higher.

## Roots

Every chord is named after one note, its **root**. A chord usually sounds best when its lowest note is the root, which is why you start the strum on a particular string. The A chord's root is the open A string, the D chord's is the open D string, and E's is the low E.

::tool[Check with the tuner]{name="tuner"}

## Major or minor?

Major chords tend to sound bright and settled; minor chords sound darker, sadder or more serious. The difference is one note in each chord.

::chords{ids="A,Am,E,Em"}

In this minor-key loop, listen to how E pulls back to Am.

::exercise{id="am-em-d-e-quarters"}

:::warning
Common mistakes:

- Mixing up the numbering: string **1** is the _thinnest_, nearest the floor.
- Judging by loudness or pitch instead of colour. A quiet major chord is still major.

:::
