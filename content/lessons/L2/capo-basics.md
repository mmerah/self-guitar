A capo is a clamp that presses all six strings at one fret. It raises the pitch of everything you play, so you can use the open-chord shapes you know in any key, for example to match a singer's voice.

## How to practise this

- Play Twinkle in G or the G–Em–C–D loop with the capo on frets 2, 3 and 5.
- Each time, work out which chords are actually sounding: count the frets up from the shape's name.
- Review: 2 minutes of D – DU – UDU on G, C and D.

## Putting it on

1. Place the capo right behind the fret wire (on the headstock side), not on top of it and not in the middle of the fret.
2. Keep it straight across the neck.
3. Close it with just enough pressure that every open string rings. Too tight pulls the strings sharp.
4. Strum and check the tuning. Retune slightly if needed.

## Shapes and sounds

With a capo you still think in shapes, but the chord that sounds is higher. Each fret raises it by one semitone (half step). With the capo on **fret 2**:

- a **G** shape sounds as **A**
- a **C** shape sounds as **D**
- a **D** shape sounds as **E**
- an **Em** shape sounds as **F♯m**
- an **Am** shape sounds as **Bm**

Chord charts usually name the shapes: "Capo 2: G, C, D" means play those shapes with the capo on fret 2.

::chords{ids="G,C,D"}

## Try it

Put the capo on fret 2 and play D – DU – UDU on G, C and D. It's the same song as before, brighter and higher. Then try the capo on fret 5: the open shapes start to sound like a mandolin.

::strum{pattern="D - D U - U D U" bpm=70 chord="G"}

::tool{name="tuner"}

:::warning
Common mistakes:

- Capo too far from the fret: strings buzz or go sharp.
- Forgetting to recheck the tuning after the capo goes on.
- Reading the sounding chord names as shapes. "Capo 2: A" in a chart means something different from "Capo 2: G shape".

:::
